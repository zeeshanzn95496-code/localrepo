EPOCH I

The Dawn of Mobility & The Name "Mercedes" (1886 – 1910)

The genesis of motor transport, Karl Benz's patented three-wheeler, Gottlieb Daimler's fast-running engines, and Wilhelm Maybach's revolutionary Mercedes 35 HP.

1886 – 1890 Chapter 01 / 28 • The Genesis

The Dawn of the Automobile & Bertha Benz's Historic Journey

Benz Patent-Motorwagen (Patent DRP 37435, 1886)

On January 29, 1886, mechanical engineer Karl Benz filed patent application DRP 37435 at the Imperial Patent Office in Berlin for his "vehicle powered by a gas engine." This official document is widely regarded by historians and UNESCO as the official birth certificate of the automobile. The Benz Patent-Motorwagen was not merely a converted carriage; it was a bespoke, integrated three-wheeled vehicle powered by a horizontal, single-cylinder four-stroke engine delivering 0.75 horsepower at 400 rpm, equipped with an electric spark ignition and water evaporative cooling.

In August 1888, Karl's wife, Bertha Benz, cemented the automobile's commercial viability without her husband's prior knowledge. Taking their sons Eugen and Richard, she undertook the world's first long-distance automobile road trip—a grueling 106-kilometer (66-mile) journey from Mannheim to Pforzheim. Demonstrating peerless ingenuity, Bertha cleaned a clogged fuel line with her hatpin, insulated an exposed ignition wire using her garter, and purchased ligroin solvent from a pharmacy in Wiesloch (which became history's first filling station). Concurrently in Bad Cannstatt, Gottlieb Daimler and Wilhelm Maybach perfected the high-speed "Grandfather Clock" engine and installed it into a carriage, laying the dual foundations of what would unite as Mercedes-Benz.

Key Milestone: Patent DRP 37435 (Jan 29, 1886) First Long-Distance Drive: Bertha Benz (106 km in 1888) Pioneers: Karl Benz, Gottlieb Daimler, Wilhelm Maybach
1891 – 1895 Chapter 02 / 28 • Four-Wheel Mass Production

The First Production Four-Wheelers: Benz Victoria & Benz Velo

Benz Velo (1894) — The World's First Serial Production Car

Having proven the viability of the three-wheeled design, Karl Benz set his sights on conquering the fundamental mechanical limitation of early four-wheeled vehicles: safe and stable directional steering. In 1893, Benz patented the double-pivot kingpin steering system (DRP 73515). This invention enabled the front wheels to turn on different radii, eradicating wheel scrub and allowing smooth, predictable cornering. The breakthrough culminated in the debut of the 1893 Benz Victoria, a robust 3-horsepower carriage that set a new benchmark for motorized transport.

In 1894, Benz took an even bolder step toward democratization by creating the Benz Velo (Velocipede). Engineered as a lightweight, reliable, and accessible automobile priced at 2,000 marks, the Velo became the world's very first series-production automobile. Over 1,200 units were manufactured between 1894 and 1902, establishing Benz & Cie. as the largest automobile manufacturer on Earth. Simultaneously in Cannstatt, Daimler-Motoren-Gesellschaft (DMG) was formally established, engineering the twin-cylinder "Phoenix" engine with Wilhelm Maybach’s pioneering spray-nozzle carburetor.

Key Patent: Double-Pivot Steering (DRP 73515) First Series Car: Benz Velo (~1,200 units built) Engine Innovation: Maybach Spray-Nozzle Carburetor
1896 – 1900 Chapter 03 / 28 • Commercial Transport & The Name "Mercedes"

Industrial Scaling, The First Truck & Emil Jellinek

Daimler Motor-Lastwagen (1896) — The World's First Commercial Truck

The closing years of the 19th century witnessed profound diversification in vehicular architecture. In October 1896, Daimler-Motoren-Gesellschaft delivered the world's first motorized truck (Motor-Lastwagen) to the British Motor Syndicate in London. Featuring a 1.06-liter rear-mounted two-cylinder Phoenix engine producing 4 horsepower and driving the rear wheels through a complex belt-and-gear mechanism, it inaugurated the modern era of road freight and logistics. Not to be outdone, Karl Benz patented the "Contra-Engine" in 1897—the world's first boxer engine with horizontally opposed cylinders, establishing a layout revered for its natural balance.

Meanwhile on the French Riviera, wealthy Austro-Hungarian diplomat and visionary entrepreneur Emil Jellinek began retailing Daimler automobiles to aristocrats and tycoons. Jellinek was an ardent motorsport enthusiast who demanded vehicles with greater speed, lower centers of gravity, and wider wheelbases. He entered races under the pseudonym "Monsieur Mercedes," named after his beloved ten-year-old daughter, Mercédès Adrienne Ramona Manuela Jellinek. In April 1900, Jellinek reached an agreement with DMG to commission a fleet of radical new high-performance cars carrying his daughter's melodic Spanish name.

Freight Pioneer: World's 1st Motorized Truck (1896) Engineering First: Benz Contra-Engine (1897 Boxer) The Name: Mercédès Jellinek inspires the brand moniker
1901 – 1905 Chapter 04 / 28 • The Architectural Revolution

The Mercedes 35 HP: Birth of the Modern Automobile

Mercedes 35 HP (1901) — The First True Modern Car

At the dawn of the 20th century, automotive engineering underwent an epochal paradigm shift. Wilhelm Maybach unveiled the Mercedes 35 HP in early 1901, purpose-built for Emil Jellinek. Up until this point, all motor cars were essentially horseless carriages—tall, narrow, top-heavy, and susceptible to overturning. Maybach discarded carriage traditions entirely, creating the definitive blueprint of the modern automobile: a low-slung, lightweight pressed-steel chassis, long wheelbase, mechanically operated intake valves, an inclined steering column, and a front-mounted 5.9-liter four-cylinder engine generating 35 horsepower.

Crucially, Maybach integrated his revolutionary honeycomb radiator directly into the car's nose, utilizing thousands of tiny soldered tubes that allowed optimal engine cooling with dramatically less water weight. At Nice Race Week in March 1901, the Mercedes 35 HP completely decimated the competition in speed trials, hill climbs, and endurance runs. Watching in awe, Paul Meyan, Secretary General of the Automobile Club of France, proclaimed the immortal words: "Nous sommes entrés dans l’ère Mercédès" ("We have entered the Mercedes era"). In 1902, DMG formally registered the brand name "Mercedes" as an official trademark.

Blueprint: Pressed-steel chassis & low center of gravity Invention: Maybach Honeycomb Radiator Trademark: "Mercedes" legally registered (1902)
1906 – 1910 Chapter 05 / 28 • The Three-Pointed Star

The Birth of the Three-Pointed Star & Worldwide Prestige

Mercedes Simplex (1905–1908) • Dawn of the Star Insignia

By 1909, Daimler-Motoren-Gesellschaft sought an unforgettable, universal emblem to visually symbolize their technical supremacy. The sons of Gottlieb Daimler (Paul and Adolf) remembered that decades earlier, their father had drawn a three-pointed star on a picture postcard of their family home in Deutz, telling his wife that this star would one day shine brightly over his flourishing factory. In June 1909, the DMG board applied for legal protection for both a three-pointed and a four-pointed star. The Three-Pointed Star was chosen, symbolizing Gottlieb Daimler's grand vision of universal motorization across land, water, and air.

In that identical year of 1909, rival Benz & Cie. registered their own storied symbol: the circular Benz name embraced by a traditional victory laurel wreath, celebrating their numerous motorsport accolades. During this period, the refined Mercedes Simplex models—featuring quiet-running valvetrains, multi-plate clutches, and opulent coachbuilt bodies—became the preferred transport of kings, emperors, and captains of industry, securing the brand's enduring reputation as the preeminent symbol of royal luxury and peerless craftsmanship.

Iconic Emblem: Three-Pointed Star registered (June 1909) Symbolism: Mastery over Land, Sea, and Sky Benz Emblem: Laurel Wreath trademark registered (1909)
EPOCH II

Speed Records, The Historic Merger & The Silver Arrows (1911 – 1935)

From the earth-shattering 200 HP Blitzen-Benz to the fusion of Daimler and Benz in 1926, Ferdinand Porsche's SSK, and the birth of the immortal Silver Arrows.

1911 – 1915 Chapter 06 / 28 • The Record Breakers

Land Speed Dominance & The 200 HP "Blitzen-Benz"

Benz 200 HP "Blitzen-Benz" (1909–1911) Land Speed King

The quest for absolute velocity reached breathtaking heights with the construction of the sensational "Blitzen-Benz" (Lightning Benz). Engineered by Benz & Cie. with aerodynamic wind-cheating bodywork, it was powered by a monumental 21.5-liter four-cylinder displacement engine—the largest engine ever fitted to a land speed record car by the company—churning out 200 horsepower. On April 23, 1911, daring American racing driver Bob Burman piloted the machine across the sands of Daytona Beach, Florida, reaching a verifiable top speed of 228.1 km/h (141.73 mph). At that moment in history, the Blitzen-Benz was twice as fast as any contemporary aircraft and decisively outpaced any high-speed steam locomotive on Earth.

Not to be overshadowed, DMG engineered an astonishing triumph at the prestigious 1914 French Grand Prix in Lyon just days before the outbreak of World War I. Running against Europe's finest marques over 750 grueling kilometers, the Mercedes team—commanded by Christian Lautenschlager, Louis Wagner, and Otto Salzer—executed a legendary 1-2-3 podium sweep in their purpose-built 4.5-liter four-cylinder race cars, featuring advanced four-valve-per-cylinder technology derived directly from aerospace engineering.

Land Speed Record: 228.1 km/h (Daytona Beach, 1911) Engine Displacement: 21.5-liter 4-cylinder (200 hp) Grand Prix Glory: 1-2-3 sweep at French GP (1914)
1916 – 1920 Chapter 07 / 28 • Aviation Metallurgy & Survival

Aviation Engines & Wartime Industrial Resilience

Mercedes 28/95 HP (1916–1920) with Aero-Derived OHC Engine

The upheaval of World War I compelled both Daimler-Motoren-Gesellschaft and Benz & Cie. to redirect their manufacturing capacities toward military logistics, heavy transport, and particularly high-performance aviation powerplants. DMG developed the renowned Mercedes D.III and D.IV aircraft engines. These inline six-cylinder engines featured cutting-edge engineering techniques: overhead camshafts driven by vertical bevel shafts, lightweight individual steel cylinder barrels welded to cooling jackets, and dual ignition systems engineered to operate under high-altitude air starvation.

The rigorous metallurgical tolerances and weight-saving breakthroughs honed during this crucible became the technical backbone for post-war civilian models. In the late 1910s, Mercedes launched the majestic Mercedes 28/95 HP, powered by a 7.3-liter six-cylinder engine directly inspired by the D.III aero-engine. Despite severe raw material shortages, hyperinflation, and economic destabilization in Weimar Germany, this model proved that mechanical excellence and ultra-luxury grand touring could endure even the darkest historical chapters.

Aviation Tech: Overhead camshafts & welded steel cylinders Post-War Flagship: Mercedes 28/95 HP (7.3-liter OHC) Durability: Aerospace metallurgy adapted for road cars
1921 – 1925 Chapter 08 / 28 • The Kompressor Revolution

The Supercharger (Kompressor) Era & Road to Merger

Supercharged Mercedes 2-Liter (1924 Targa Florio Victor)

In 1921, Daimler-Motoren-Gesellschaft shocked the automotive world at the Berlin Motor Show by presenting the world's very first series-production passenger automobiles equipped with engine-driven mechanical superchargers: the Mercedes 6/25/40 HP and 10/40/65 HP. Driven by Roots-type blowers engaged by depressing the accelerator pedal fully to the floor, the supercharger forced pressurized air into the combustion chambers with a spine-tingling mechanical scream, dramatically increasing power output by over 60 percent.

In 1923, brilliant Austrian engineer Dr. Ferdinand Porsche was appointed Chief Designer at DMG in Untertürkheim. Porsche immediately supercharged the two-liter racing car, leading Christian Werner to a celebrated victory at the grueling 1924 Sicilian Targa Florio. Yet the devastating hyperinflation and economic crisis in Germany threatened the survival of independent automakers. Recognizing their shared heritage and complementary strengths, DMG and Benz & Cie. signed an agreement of mutual interest in May 1924, combining design, production, and marketing operations—the decisive stepping stone to full unification.

World First: Roots superchargers on production cars (1921) Chief Engineer: Dr. Ferdinand Porsche joins DMG (1923) Historic Alliance: DMG and Benz & Cie. syndicate formed (1924)
1926 – 1930 Chapter 09 / 28 • The Historic Fusion

The Historic Merger: Daimler-Benz AG & The Mighty SSK

Mercedes-Benz SSK (1928) • Engineered by Ferdinand Porsche

On June 28, 1926, the two pioneering automotive titans of Germany officially consummated their historic merger, creating Daimler-Benz AG with headquarters in Stuttgart-Untertürkheim. The newly born automobile marque was christened Mercedes-Benz. To commemorate the union, a brand-new trademark emblem was forged: Daimler’s three-pointed star nestled inside a circular ring, embraced by the classic Benz laurel wreath and bearing the names "Mercedes" and "Benz".

Ferdinand Porsche celebrated the merger by creating the awe-inspiring "White Elephants" series: the S (Sport), SS (Super Sport), and the immortal SSK (Super Sport Kurz) in 1928. Built on a shortened 2.95-meter wheelbase with a massive 7.1-liter supercharged inline-six delivering up to 300 horsepower when the blower screamed to life, the SSK achieved an astonishing 192 km/h (120 mph). In 1931, racing ace Rudolf Caracciola piloted an SSK to victory at the grueling 1,000-mile Italian Mille Miglia, making him the first non-Italian driver ever to win the road-racing classic.

The Birth: Daimler-Benz AG formally established (June 28, 1926) United Logo: Three-pointed star merged with Benz laurel wreath Supercar Icon: Mercedes-Benz SSK (7.1-liter, 300 hp kompressor)
1931 – 1935 Chapter 10 / 28 • The Silver Arrows Are Born

Independent Suspension & The First "Silver Arrow" (W25)

Mercedes-Benz W25 (1934) • The Original Silver Arrow

In 1931, Mercedes-Benz revolutionized passenger ride quality by introducing the Mercedes-Benz Typ 170 (W15). It was the world's first volume-produced car equipped with four-wheel independent suspension—utilizing front transverse leaf springs and a rear swing axle with coil springs. This suspension geometry virtually eliminated axle hop, providing ride comfort and roadholding so far ahead of contemporary rigid-axle vehicles that it established an engineering standard copied worldwide.

In 1934, international Grand Prix racing introduced the 750-kg maximum dry weight formula to curb excessive speeds. Mercedes-Benz responded with the radical W25, powered by a 3.4-liter supercharged straight-eight delivering 354 hp. At the Eifelrennen race on the Nürburgring in June 1934, the W25 weighed in at 751 kg—exactly one kilogram over the limit. Team manager Alfred Neubauer and driver Manfred von Brauchitsch ordered mechanics to scrape the traditional white lead paint down to the bare polished aluminum skin overnight. The sparkling silver car won the race, and radio journalists christened the cars the "Silver Arrows" (Silberpfeile), launching motorsport's greatest dynasty.

Chassis Breakthrough: 4-wheel independent suspension on Typ 170 (1931) Motorsport Legend: Birth of the "Silver Arrows" (W25, 1934) Championship: Rudolf Caracciola takes 1935 European Championship
EPOCH III

The Diesel Pioneer, Devastation, Rebirth & The Gullwing (1936 – 1960)

The world's first diesel passenger car, 432 km/h autobahn records, destruction in WWII, rising from the rubble, the legendary 300 SL Gullwing, and crumple zones.

1936 – 1940 Chapter 11 / 28 • The Diesel Pioneer & Absolute Speed

The First Diesel Passenger Car & The 432.7 km/h Autobahn Record

Mercedes-Benz 260 D (1936) • World's First Diesel Passenger Car

In February 1936 at the Berlin Motor Show, Mercedes-Benz made global automotive history by unveiling the Mercedes-Benz 260 D (W138)—the world's very first serial-production diesel-powered passenger automobile. Powered by a 2.54-liter four-cylinder diesel engine utilizing Bosch pre-chamber injection and producing 45 horsepower, the 260 D consumed over 30 percent less fuel than equivalent petrol cars. It became an immediate legend for taxi fleets and long-distance drivers, establishing diesel as a long-range, indestructible propulsion option.

At the opposite end of the spectrum, Mercedes-Benz chased aerodynamic perfection on public highways. On January 28, 1938, Rudolf Caracciola strapped into the streamliner Mercedes-Benz W125 Rekordwagen on the newly paved Frankfurt-Darmstadt Autobahn. Powered by a twin-supercharged 5.6-liter V12 producing 736 hp and encased in a wind-tunnel-optimized body with ice-cooled radiator, Caracciola clocked 432.7 km/h (268.8 mph) over a flying kilometer. This staggering record for a public road speed run stood unbroken for nearly 80 years. Simultaneously, the majestic 540 K Special Roadster ruled as the zenith of bespoke pre-war grand touring.

World First: Mercedes-Benz 260 D diesel passenger car (1936) Speed Record: 432.7 km/h on public highway (W125, Jan 1938) Luxury Pinnacle: Mercedes-Benz 540 K Special Roadster
1941 – 1945 Chapter 12 / 28 • The War Years & Devastation

The Devastating War Years & Total Destruction

Mercedes-Benz 170 V (W136) • The Backbone That Survived the War

With the eruption of total war, Daimler-Benz AG was forcefully absorbed into the wartime command economy. Civilian passenger car development was abruptly halted in 1942, and production was converted entirely to military vehicles, medium trucks (such as the L 3000), aircraft engines (including the inverted-V12 DB 601 and DB 605 with direct mechanical fuel injection for Messerschmitt fighters), and tracked military transports. The robust four-cylinder Mercedes-Benz 170 V (W136) chassis served as the mechanical workhorse for ambulances, staff cars, and gas-generator utility vehicles.

By 1944 and 1945, the consequences of war struck home with catastrophic devastation. Allied bombing raids targeted the core production complexes in Untertürkheim, Sindelfingen, Mannheim, and Gaggenau. Two-thirds of the buildings in Untertürkheim were leveled; 85 percent of the Sindelfingen body plant lay in smoldering ruins; Gaggenau was virtually obliterated. When hostilities ceased in May 1945, the Daimler-Benz board gravely assessed that the corporation had physically ceased to exist as an operating manufacturing concern.

Wartime Reality: Conversion to military logistics & DB 601 aero engines Devastation: Over 80% of primary manufacturing facilities destroyed Survival Tooling: 170 V dies preserved amid the rubble
1946 – 1950 Chapter 13 / 28 • The Rebirth from the Ashes

Miraculous Rebirth: The 170 V Rekindles the Flame

Mercedes-Benz 170 V & 170 S (1947–1950) • The Post-War Economic Miracle

Amid the smoking rubble of post-war Germany, surviving factory workers returned with shovels and wheelbarrows to clear debris with their bare hands. In May 1946, the American military occupation government granted Daimler-Benz permission to resume limited industrial activities to build utility vehicles essential for survival. Scavenging intact machine tools from bomb shelters, workers restarted production of the reliable Mercedes-Benz 170 V (W136), producing basic platform trucks, delivery panel vans, and emergency ambulances.

In July 1947, regular passenger sedan production officially restarted—a watershed moment marking Germany’s industrial rebirth. In 1949, Mercedes introduced two pivotal models: the luxurious 170 S (featuring a wider body, telescopic shock absorbers, and modernized front suspension) and the economical 170 D (reintroducing passenger diesel frugality to fuel Europe's post-war reconstruction). Against all rational odds, Daimler-Benz had not only survived extinction; it had laid the groundwork for the German Economic Miracle (Wirtschaftswunder).

Reconstruction: Production reauthorized by Allied forces (May 1946) First Post-War Cars: 170 V delivery vans, ambulances & sedans Diesel Revival: 170 D launched (1949) to power reconstruction
1951 – 1955 Chapter 14 / 28 • The Golden Era

The Golden Era: The 300 SL "Gullwing" & Juan Manuel Fangio

Mercedes-Benz 300 SL Gullwing (W198, 1954) • Automotive Legend

The 1950s heralded the greatest golden age in Mercedes-Benz history. In 1951, the majestic 300 "Adenauer" saloon re-established Stuttgart at the absolute peak of statesman luxury. But it was chief test engineer Rudolf Uhlenhaut who shook the world in 1952 with the lightweight tubular spaceframe 300 SL (W194) racing car, conquering the 24 Hours of Le Mans, the Nürburgring, and the brutal Carrera Panamericana. Encouraged by American luxury car importer Max Hoffman, Daimler-Benz introduced the road-going 300 SL "Gullwing" (W198) at the 1954 New York Auto Show.

The 300 SL was an engineering tour-de-force: its high sills required upward-opening gullwing doors, while its 3.0-liter dry-sump inline-six featured mechanical direct fuel injection (a global first for production cars) producing 215 hp and a top speed of 260 km/h (161 mph)—making it the undisputed fastest production sports car of its time. Simultaneously, legendary Argentine maestro Juan Manuel Fangio piloted the sublime W196 Silver Arrow to consecutive Formula 1 World Championships in 1954 and 1955, while Stirling Moss and Denis Jenkinson conquered the 1955 Mille Miglia in the 300 SLR #722 with an immortal record time of 10 hours, 7 minutes, and 48 seconds.

Supercar Masterpiece: 300 SL Gullwing with direct fuel injection (1954) F1 World Champion: Juan Manuel Fangio in W196 (1954, 1955) Mille Miglia Record: Stirling Moss in 300 SLR #722 (1955)
1956 – 1960 Chapter 15 / 28 • The Safety Revolution

The Safety Revolution: Béla Barényi's Crumple Zones & The "Fintail"

Mercedes-Benz W111 "Fintail" (Heckflosse, 1959) • First Crumple Zone Car

Prior to 1959, automotive designers universally believed that a car should be as rigid and unyielding as an armored tank to protect its occupants. Hungarian-born Mercedes engineer Béla Barényi completely shattered this dogmatic fallacy. Barényi recognized that during an impact, kinetic energy must be absorbed by the vehicle itself rather than transferred directly into human bodies. In 1951, he patented the fundamental principle of passive vehicular safety (Patent DRP 854157): a rigid passenger safety cell flanked by front and rear impact-absorbing crumple zones (Knautschzone).

In autumn 1959, this life-saving concept entered series production with the elegant Mercedes-Benz W111 "Fintail" (Heckflosse). Beyond its crumple zones, the W111 featured a steering wheel with a large padded hub, recessed interior switches, breakaway dashboard elements, and factory seat belt mounting points. To validate their calculations, Mercedes engineers performed the industry's very first systematic crash tests against concrete barriers in Sindelfingen in September 1959. Barényi's crumple zone invention has since saved millions of human lives across all vehicle manufacturers globally.

Life-Saving Invention: Béla Barényi's Crumple Zones (Patent DRP 854157) Serial Debut: W111 "Fintail" Heckflosse (1959) Industry First: Systematic barrier crash testing instituted at Sindelfingen
EPOCH IV

The Grand 600, The Birth of AMG & The Safety Vanguard (1961 – 1985)

The colossal 600 Pullman, the Pagoda SL, the founding of AMG and the 300 SEL 6.3 "Red Pig", the official S-Class moniker, W123 immortality, and ABS.

1961 – 1965 Chapter 16 / 28 • Sovereign Grandeur & The Pagoda

The Grand 600 Pullman & Paul Bracq's Iconic "Pagoda" SL

Mercedes-Benz 600 "Grand Mercedes" (W100, 1963)

In 1963 at the Frankfurt Motor Show, Mercedes-Benz unleashed the pinnacle of twentieth-century statecraft luxury: the Mercedes-Benz 600 "Grand Mercedes" (W100). Available as a limousine or extended six-door Pullman, the 600 was powered by the brand’s first post-war V8 engine: the massive 6.3-liter M100 producing 250 hp. To ensure whisper-quiet operation without clunky electric motors, Mercedes engineered a complex 150-bar (2,175 psi) hydraulic system that silently operated power windows, seats, fresh-air vents, sunroof, and automatic self-closing doors. The "Grosser" became the definitive chariot for Elizabeth II, John F. Kennedy, Elvis Presley, and heads of state worldwide.

Simultaneously in 1963, French design maestro Paul Bracq penned the breathtaking Mercedes-Benz 230 SL (W113). Replacing both the fragile 190 SL and the ultra-expensive 300 SL roadster, the car earned the affectionate nickname "Pagoda" due to its distinctive concave removable hardtop. This architectural roofline was not merely a stylistic flourish; its raised outer edges and dipped center dramatically enlarged window visibility, facilitated easier passenger ingress, and vastly increased roll-over roof rigidity.

Chariot of Kings: Mercedes-Benz 600 Pullman (6.3L V8 M100) Silent Comfort: 150-bar central hydraulic convenience system Design Masterpiece: W113 230 SL "Pagoda" by Paul Bracq
1966 – 1970 Chapter 17 / 28 • The Genesis of AMG

The Genesis of AMG & The "Red Pig" 300 SEL 6.3

Mercedes-Benz 300 SEL 6.3 (W109, 1968) • Genesis of Modern AMG

In 1967, in an old mill in Burgstall an der Murr, two visionary former Mercedes-Benz racing department engineers—Hans Werner Aufrecht and Erhard Melcher—founded an independent engineering company specializing in competition engine development. They combined their initials with Aufrecht’s birthplace (Großaspach) to create AMG. Their mission was singular: to transform refined Mercedes sedans into ferociously fast racing thoroughbreds.

Concurrently within Mercedes-Benz, brilliant engineer Erich Waxenberger conducted a daring skunkworks experiment. Without management approval, he shoehorned the colossal 6.3-liter M100 V8 from the 600 limousine into the lightweight Paul Bracq-designed W109 saloon. When development chief Rudolf Uhlenhaut took it for a test drive, he was captivated. The resulting Mercedes-Benz 300 SEL 6.3 debuted at Geneva in 1968 as the undisputed fastest four-door production sedan in the world (0–100 km/h in 6.5 seconds). AMG then bored this engine out to 6.8 liters (428 hp) to create the legendary "Rote Sau" (Red Pig), scoring an improbable 2nd place overall at the 1971 24 Hours of Spa and etching AMG into racing lore forever.

Affalterbach Genesis: Aufrecht, Melcher & Großaspach found AMG (1967) Super Sedan: 300 SEL 6.3 (fastest 4-door sedan of its day) Racing Legend: "Rote Sau" (Red Pig) 300 SEL 6.8 wins 24h Spa podium
1971 – 1975 Chapter 18 / 28 • The S-Class Moniker

The Evergreen R107 SL & The Official "S-Class" (W116)

Mercedes-Benz W116 (1972) • The Official Debut of the "S-Class"

In 1971, Mercedes-Benz launched the immortal R107 SL Roadster and its fixed-roof sibling C107 SLC. Featuring distinctive ribbed taillights designed to channel dirt and rainwater away from the lenses, an impact-absorbing four-spoke safety steering wheel, and high-tensile A-pillars, the R107 became a symbol of effortless Hollywood and Riviera glamour. It proved so mechanically refined and universally admired that it stayed in production for an astounding 18 years until 1989.

In 1972, Mercedes-Benz took an epochal marketing and engineering step by formally designating its flagship executive saloon as the S-Class (Sonderklasse / Special Class) with the debut of the W116. The W116 established unprecedented safety standards: its collision-protected fuel tank was relocated above the rear axle rather than under the trunk, and side impact intrusion beams reinforced every door. The series reached its absolute zenith with the 1975 450 SEL 6.9, featuring a hand-assembled 6.9-liter V8, dry-sump lubrication, and self-leveling hydropneumatic suspension that absorbed every imperfection on the road.

Official Moniker: "S-Class" (Sonderklasse) debuted with W116 (1972) Evergreen Roadster: R107 SL (18-year production run, 1971–1989) Autobahn Stormer: 450 SEL 6.9 with hydropneumatic suspension
1976 – 1980 Chapter 19 / 28 • Unbreakable Immortality & ABS

Unbreakable Reliability: W123, The G-Wagen & ABS Premiere

Mercedes-Benz W123 (1976) • The Most Reliable Car in History

In January 1976, Mercedes-Benz rolled out the W123 series, universally regarded by mechanics and historians as the most robust, over-engineered automobile in automotive history. Customers clamored so aggressively for the car that delivery waiting lists stretched to three years, with secondhand models trading for above sticker price. Nearly 2.7 million units were produced across saloon, coupe, and estate variants (the S123, the brand's first factory station wagon). Powered by naturally aspirated diesels like the immortal 240D and 300D, hundreds of thousands of W123 taxis routinely crossed one million and even two million kilometers without major overhauls.

Two other monumental milestones defined this era. In 1978, Mercedes-Benz and Bosch shocked the automotive world by presenting the world's very first digital multi-channel Anti-lock Braking System (ABS) as an option on the W116 S-Class, ensuring full steering control even during maximum emergency braking on ice. A year later, in 1979, the rugged Geländewagen (G-Class) was born in Graz, Austria. Developed initially for extreme military and cross-country operations with three locking differentials, the G-Wagen embarked on a four-decade evolution to become a premier luxury cultural icon.

Indestructible Legend: W123 series (2.7 million built, 1,000,000+ km longevity) Active Safety Breakthrough: World's 1st digital ABS with Bosch (1978) Off-Road Icon: Mercedes-Benz G-Class (Geländewagen) born (1979)
1981 – 1985 Chapter 20 / 28 • The Baby Benz & Airbags

The "Baby Benz" 190 E (W201), Multi-Link Suspension & Airbags

Mercedes-Benz 190 E (W201, 1982) • The "Baby Benz"

In 1981, Mercedes-Benz achieved another monumental milestone in automotive safety: it became the very first European automaker to offer a driver front airbag combined with pyrotechnic seatbelt pretensioners on the flagship W126 S-Class. Through complex micro-sensor triggering, the bag deployed in less than 30 milliseconds, dramatically reducing severe head injuries in frontal collisions.

Then in December 1982, Mercedes entered the compact executive segment with the radical 190 / 190 E (W201), affectionately hailed as the "Baby Benz". Developed with a staggering budget of over \$600 million, design director Bruno Sacco penned a wedge-shaped aerodynamic profile (Cd 0.33) with lightweight high-strength steels. Crucially, the W201 debuted the revolutionary five-link multi-link rear suspension, where each rear wheel was controlled by five separate links to optimize lateral, longitudinal, and camber forces. In August 1983, three Cosworth-developed 190 E 2.3-16 models ran non-stop around the high-speed Nardò test track in Italy for 201 hours, shattering 12 world endurance records by averaging 247.9 km/h over 50,000 kilometers.

Passive Safety: First European driver airbag & belt pretensioner (1981) Chassis Revolution: Five-link multi-link rear suspension on W201 Endurance Record: 190 E 2.3-16 averages 247.9 km/h over 50,000 km
EPOCH V

Electronics, Global Expansion & Supercar Mastery (1986 – 2010)

1986 – 1990 Chapter 21 / 28 • The Wolf in Sheep's Clothing

4MATIC All-Wheel Drive, The R129 SL & The 500 E "Iron Fist"

Mercedes-Benz 500 E (W124, 1990) • Hand-Crafted with Porsche

At the 1985 Frankfurt show, Mercedes-Benz revealed the dynamic driving revolution: 4MATIC, its first electronically controlled all-wheel-drive system, introduced on the acclaimed W124 E-Class. 4MATIC used sophisticated wheel-speed sensors and hydraulic clutches to switch seamlessly between rear-wheel drive and four-wheel drive with locking differentials in milliseconds. In 1989, Bruno Sacco unleashed the peerless R129 SL roadster, featuring an electro-hydraulic soft top that folded away in 30 seconds, magnesium-framed integral seats, and an automatic sensor-triggered pop-up roll bar that snapped upright in 0.3 seconds if an impending rollover was detected.

In 1990, Mercedes-Benz partnered with neighboring sports car marque Porsche to engineer the ultimate high-performance sports sedan: the Mercedes-Benz 500 E (W124). Nicknamed the "Wolf in Sheep’s Clothing" or the "Iron Fist in a Velvet Glove", the 500 E was equipped with the 5.0-liter 32-valve M119 V8 from the SL, delivering 322 horsepower and 0–100 km/h in 5.9 seconds. Because its muscular flared wheel arches were too wide for the Sindelfingen assembly line, each car was transported back and forth and hand-assembled at Porsche’s Rössle-Bau plant in Zuffenhausen over 18 days.

Traction Tech: 4MATIC electronic all-wheel drive launched Open-Top Safety: R129 SL with 0.3-second automatic pop-up roll bar (1989) Cult Hero: 500 E hand-assembled by Porsche (322 hp V8)
1991 – 1995 Chapter 22 / 28 • The Cathedral S-Class & ESP

The "Cathedral" W140 V12, ESP Innovation & Modern Naming

Mercedes-Benz W140 S-Class (1991) • The Ultimate "Cathedral"

In 1991, Mercedes-Benz unveiled the monumental W140 S-Class, an engineering triumph colloquially known across Germany as the "Cathedral" (Der Dom). Uncompromised in its scope, the W140 introduced double-pane acoustic insulated glass to prevent condensation and eliminate wind noise, pneumatic servo closing aids that silently sucked doors and trunk shut, retracting parking guide rods, and CAN-bus digital multiplex wiring. Crucially, the W140 introduced the brand's first-ever production passenger car V12 engine: the 6.0-liter M120 producing a formidable 394 hp and 570 Nm of torque in the flagship 600 SEL.

In 1993, Mercedes-Benz streamlined its entire global model hierarchy, introducing the modern alphanumeric naming system where letter classes (C-Class, E-Class, S-Class) preceded displacement numerals. Then in 1995, Mercedes-Benz and Bosch achieved what many consider the single greatest active safety advancement since the seatbelt: the Electronic Stability Program (ESP). Debuting on the S 600 Coupé, ESP monitored steering angle, lateral acceleration, and yaw rate, automatically applying individual brakes to tame understeer and oversteer and keep vehicles on their intended path.

First V12: 6.0L M120 V12 (394 hp) in flagship 600 SEL Active Safety Breakthrough: Electronic Stability Program (ESP, 1995) Nomenclature: Modern C-Class, E-Class, S-Class format adopted (1993)
1996 – 2000 Chapter 23 / 28 • Global Expansion & The Vario-Roof

Global Expansion: SLK Vario-Roof, A-Class & M-Class Luxury SUV

Mercedes-Benz SLK (R170, 1996) • The Vario-Roof Roadster

The late 1990s witnessed the boldest portfolio expansion in the marque’s history. In 1996, the compact Mercedes-Benz SLK (R170) ignited a global sensation by popularizing the electro-hydraulic folding steel "Vario-roof". In just 25 seconds, the SLK transformed from a weather-tight luxury coupe into an open-air roadster, rendering conventional canvas soft-tops obsolete. In 1997, the tall-roof A-Class (W168) proved that frontal safety could be achieved in a compact car through an ingenious "sandwich floor" concept that slid the engine beneath the passenger cabin in a severe impact.

Also in 1997, Mercedes-Benz pioneered the modern premium luxury SUV market with the M-Class (W163), constructed at a new state-of-the-art manufacturing plant in Tuscaloosa, Alabama. Armed with permanent 4MATIC and the 4-ETS electronic traction system, the ML blended car-like comfort with off-road prowess. On the corporate front, in November 1998, Daimler-Benz CEO Jürgen Schrempp orchestrated the \$36 billion "merger of equals" with Chrysler Corporation to form DaimlerChrysler AG, creating a transatlantic industrial colossus.

Convertible Revolution: SLK folding steel Vario-roof (1996) Luxury SUV Era: Alabama-built M-Class ML 320 (W163, 1997) Transatlantic Merger: DaimlerChrysler AG formed (1998)
2001 – 2005 Chapter 24 / 28 • Carbon-Fiber Supercars & Maybach

Carbon-Fiber Supercars (SLR McLaren) & Maybach Rebirth

Mercedes-Benz SLR McLaren (2003) • 617 HP Carbon-Fiber Supercar

The dawn of the 21st century saw Mercedes-Benz push the boundaries of extreme supercar performance and bespoke ultra-luxury. In 2003, in collaboration with Formula One partner McLaren in Woking, England, the company introduced the Mercedes-Benz SLR McLaren (C199). Inspired by the 1955 300 SLR Uhlenhaut Coupé, the SLR featured a hand-laid carbon-fiber composite monocoque, carbon-ceramic brake discs, side-exit exhausts, and a 617-horsepower hand-built 5.4-liter supercharged M155 V8 capable of 334 km/h (208 mph), paired with an active rear spoiler that doubled as an airbrake.

Simultaneously, Daimler revived the fabled pre-war luxury marque Maybach with the Maybach 57 and long-wheelbase Maybach 62. These bespoke super-limousines offered first-class fully reclining airline seats, electro-transparent panoramic glass roofs, 600-watt Bose surround audio, and a 5.5-liter twin-turbo V12. In 2002, the W211 E-Class debuted the revolutionary PRE-SAFE anticipatory occupant protection system, which detected impending collisions via stability sensors and automatically pre-tensioned seatbelts and closed the sunroof and windows before impact.

F1 Supercar: Mercedes-Benz SLR McLaren (617 hp, 334 km/h top speed) Ultra-Luxury: Rebirth of the Maybach brand (Maybach 57 & 62) Predictive Safety: PRE-SAFE anticipatory occupant protection (2002)
2006 – 2010 Chapter 25 / 28 • The Purebred SLS AMG

The In-House SLS AMG Gullwing & Modern Restructuring

Mercedes-Benz SLS AMG (C197, 2009) • The First Bespoke AMG Supercar

In 2007, under the leadership of Dr. Dieter Zetsche, Daimler divested the Chrysler Corporation to focus completely on its core strengths: engineering perfection, luxury, and technology under the re-christened corporate entity Daimler AG. On the environmental front, the company introduced BlueTEC clean diesel technology, using AdBlue urea exhaust treatment to eliminate up to 80% of nitrogen oxides, enabling diesels to meet the most stringent 50-state emissions standards in North America.

Then in 2009 at Frankfurt, Mercedes-AMG stunned the supercar elite by revealing the SLS AMG (C197). Unlike previous AMG models developed from Mercedes production sedans, the SLS was designed, engineered, and developed from a clean sheet entirely within AMG headquarters in Affalterbach. Paying homage to the legendary 1954 300 SL with upward-swinging gullwing doors, it featured an all-aluminum spaceframe chassis, a rear transaxle dual-clutch transmission, and the glorious naturally aspirated 6.2-liter M159 V8 generating 563 hp with an intoxicating acoustic howl. It established Mercedes-AMG as a standalone sports car manufacturer of the highest echelon.

First Bespoke AMG: SLS AMG Gullwing (6.2L V8 M159, 563 hp) Clean Emissions: BlueTEC selective catalytic reduction (AdBlue) Corporate Focus: Divestiture of Chrysler to focus purely on luxury
EPOCH VI

2011 – 2015 Chapter 26 / 28 • Sensual Purity & F1 Reign

"Sensual Purity" Design, The W222 S-Class & F1 Dynasty

Mercedes-Benz W222 S-Class (2013) • The Benchmark of Luxury

Under Chief Design Officer Gorden Wagener, Mercedes-Benz inaugurated its defining aesthetic doctrine: "Sensual Purity". Replacing rigid boxy creases with clean, continuous flowing surfaces and sculpted athletic proportions, this design ethos rejuvenated the entire portfolio from the compact A-Class to the grandest limousines. In 2013, the benchmark W222 S-Class debuted. It was the world's first automobile without a single incandescent bulb—utilizing over 500 high-efficiency LEDs inside and out—and introduced ROAD SURFACE SCAN paired with MAGIC BODY CONTROL, using stereo windshield cameras to read road bumps ahead and adjust the hydraulic suspension before the wheels even struck the obstacle.

In 2014, Formula One underwent its biggest technical revolution in history with the transition to 1.6-liter turbocharged V6 hybrid power units. Having spent years perfecting thermal efficiency, the Mercedes-AMG Petronas F1 Team unleashed the devastatingly fast F1 W05 Hybrid. Piloted by Lewis Hamilton and Nico Rosberg, the Silver Arrows won 16 out of 19 races, clinching the 2014 Constructors’ and Drivers’ World Championships. This triumph inaugurated an unprecedented reign of eight consecutive Formula One World Constructors' Championships (2014–2021), the longest unbroken dynasty in motorsport history.

Design Philosophy: Sensual Purity aesthetic launched Flagship Tech: W222 S-Class with 100% LED lighting & MAGIC BODY CONTROL F1 Dominance: Start of 8 consecutive F1 World Championships (2014)
2016 – 2020 Chapter 27 / 28 • The Digital Cockpit Revolution

The Digital Cockpit Era: MBUX AI, EQ Vision & The AMG ONE

Mercedes-AMG Concept & Formula 1-Derived Hypercar Era

In 2018 at CES in Las Vegas, Mercedes-Benz revolutionized the human-machine interface by presenting MBUX (Mercedes-Benz User Experience). Powered by artificial intelligence, high-resolution widescreen displays, augmented reality navigation, and natural speech recognition triggered by saying "Hey Mercedes", the system learned its driver’s habits, predicted radio stations or phone calls, and adjusted climate controls seamlessly through intuitive conversation.

At the 2016 Paris Motor Show, the company unveiled its electric master plan with the EQ ("Electric Intelligence") sub-brand, launching its first dedicated production electric SUV, the EQC, in 2019. Meanwhile, Mercedes-AMG embarked on the most audacious road-car engineering project in history: the Mercedes-AMG ONE hypercar. Engineers successfully transplanted the genuine 1.6-liter turbocharged V6 hybrid engine straight out of Lewis Hamilton's championship-winning Formula One car into a road-legal machine. Revving to 11,000 rpm and delivering over 1,063 horsepower via four electric motors and active aerodynamics, the AMG ONE smashed the production car lap record at the Nürburgring Nordschleife.

2021 – 2026 Chapter 28 / 28 • The Electric Renaissance

The Electric Renaissance & Level 3 Certified Autonomy

Mercedes-Benz Vision EQXX & Dedicated Electric Architecture (2022–2026)

Entering its 14th decade of leadership, Mercedes-Benz launched the purpose-built Electric Vehicle Architecture (EVA) spearheaded by the flagship EQS Sedan. Boasting a world-record production aerodynamic drag coefficient of just Cd 0.20, a 108 kWh battery pack with over 770 km range, and the colossal 56-inch curved glass pillar-to-pillar MBUX Hyperscreen, the EQS redefined zero-emission luxury. In 2022, the research prototype Vision EQXX shattered electric efficiency barriers, completing over 1,202 kilometers (747 miles) from Stuttgart to Silverstone on a single battery charge with real-world energy consumption of under 8.3 kWh per 100 km.

Most remarkably, in 2022–2024 Mercedes-Benz achieved what no other manufacturer on the planet had accomplished: becoming the world’s first automaker to receive internationally certified regulatory approval for Level 3 automated driving (DRIVE PILOT). Utilizing LiDAR, radar, cameras, moisture sensors, and ultra-precise GPS, DRIVE PILOT legally allows the driver to take their hands off the wheel and eyes off the road in conditionally automated highway traffic. As the company pioneers the next-generation modular MMA platform, Concept CLA Class with 800V fast-charging, bespoke Mercedes-Maybach electric limousines, and dedicated high-performance AMG electric architectures, Mercedes-Benz continues to fulfill Gottlieb Daimler's timeless founding promise: "The Best or Nothing."
